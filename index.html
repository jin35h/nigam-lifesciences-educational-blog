Does Drinking Water Moisturise Your Skin?

You drink water for your health. You apply moisturiser for your skin. But are they doing the same job? Let’s separate the popular skincare myth from what research actually tells us.

The short answer

Water hydrates your body. Moisturiser supports your skin barrier.

Drinking enough water is important for normal body functions. It may also have a modest effect on skin hydration, particularly if your usual fluid intake is low. But drinking extra water is not a substitute for applying a suitable moisturiser to dry skin.

Myth vs fact

MYTH: “If I drink more water, I won’t need moisturiser.”
FACT: They serve different purposes. Adequate fluid intake supports overall hydration; moisturisers help keep the outer skin layer hydrated and reduce moisture loss.

01 · Understand the difference

What actually happens when you drink water?

Water is essential for circulation, temperature regulation, digestion and many other physiological processes. Your body distributes and regulates fluid according to its needs. Skin is one part of this larger system, but drinking a glass of water does not work like applying a moisturising cream directly to the skin.

Research suggests that increasing water intake may slightly improve some measures of skin hydration, especially among people who previously consumed less water. However, the evidence base is limited, and it does not establish that drinking extra water reliably treats dry skin or replaces skincare.

02 · What does research say?

Evidence: promising, but not a magic fix

A 2018 systematic review assessed studies on dietary water intake and skin properties. The authors reported that the available evidence was limited in quantity and quality. Some studies suggested a small increase in skin hydration after additional water intake, particularly among people with lower baseline consumption. The review did not establish that drinking more water prevents or cures dry skin.

A 2024 study involving 43 healthy female participants compared additional water intake and moisturiser use. Its findings suggested that moisturiser application had a more favourable impact on measured skin hydration than additional water intake. The study was relatively small, so its results should be interpreted with appropriate caution rather than treated as a universal rule.

What this means for everyday life: drink enough fluids for your body's needs, but do not force excessive water intake in the hope of changing your skin. If dryness is the concern, a suitable topical routine is usually the more direct place to start.

03 · The skin barrier explained

Why does your skin need a moisturiser?

The outermost layer of skin, called the stratum corneum, helps form a protective barrier. It helps limit water loss and protects against everyday environmental exposure. When this barrier is disrupted or the skin becomes dry, it may feel rough, tight, flaky or itchy.

Moisturiser formulas can combine different types of ingredients. Their roles are not identical:

Humectants — attract water

Ingredients such as glycerin and hyaluronic acid help draw and hold water in the outer skin layer.

Emollients — soften the surface

These help smooth rough areas by filling spaces between dry skin cells, improving the feel of the skin.

Occlusives — reduce water loss

Ingredients such as petrolatum can form a protective layer that slows water evaporation from the skin.

Different products use different ingredient combinations. The best choice depends on your skin type, the area being treated, personal tolerance and the product's directions.

04 · Simple routine

Practical habits that support comfortable skin

01 · Drink according to your needs

Maintain regular fluid intake. Needs vary with activity, climate, diet and health conditions.

02 · Keep bathing gentle

Prefer short, warm showers over long, very hot baths when your skin is dry.

03 · Moisturise after bathing

Apply a suitable moisturiser soon after bathing while skin is slightly damp, as directed.

04 · Choose a gentle cleanser

Avoid harsh cleansing and excessive scrubbing that can aggravate dryness.

05 · Notice your skin's response

Introduce new products carefully. Stop using a product if it causes persistent irritation.

06 · Seek help when needed

Persistent, painful, cracked, bleeding or inflamed skin deserves advice from a qualified clinician.

These habits are general education, not a personalised treatment plan. Dermatology guidance may differ for eczema, allergies, infections and other skin conditions.

05 · Frequently asked questions

Quick answers to common questions

Does drinking water moisturise your skin?

Drinking enough fluids supports overall hydration and may modestly influence skin hydration, especially in people who usually drink little. It does not replace a topical moisturiser, which helps reduce water loss from the skin.

Can drinking more water cure dry skin?

No. Drinking more water than your body needs is not a proven treatment for dry skin. Dryness can have many causes and may need gentle skincare or medical advice.

When should I apply moisturiser?

For dry skin, dermatologists commonly advise applying moisturiser soon after bathing while the skin is still slightly damp, following the product label.

Are water and moisturiser doing the same job?

No. Fluids support hydration throughout the body. Moisturisers act on the skin surface by attracting, softening or helping limit the loss of water.

Should I drink a fixed number of glasses of water for glowing skin?

There is no universal water target that guarantees glowing skin. Fluid needs vary between people. Follow appropriate health guidance for your circumstances rather than forcing excess water intake.

When should I consult a dermatologist?

Seek professional advice if dryness is persistent, severe, painful, cracked, bleeding, associated with a rash, or not improving with gentle care.
